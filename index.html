Professional work · Thread Bank

AI agents I built in BaaS operations

As an analyst on the Banking-as-a-Service team, I kept running into the same problem. The information people needed was sitting in inboxes and long documents, and someone had to dig it out by hand, over and over. So I built a number of internal AI agents to do that digging for them. Together they saved the team more than ten hours a week. The three below are examples. They stay high level, with partner names and specific numbers left out.

Legal Agreement Review Agent

A live view of every partner contract, for the leadership team.

The problem
We had legal agreements with a lot of partners, and each one buried the important details inside a long PDF: when it expired, what each side had agreed to do, and what fees were owed in each direction. Answering a simple question meant someone opening the document and reading through it.
What it does
It reads each agreement, identifies the key terms (expiration and renewal dates, fee schedules, and the obligations on both sides), and writes them into a structured record. Those records feed an app hosted on AWS that shows every contract side by side and stays current as agreements are added or amended.
Who uses it
The executive team. Instead of digging through PDFs, leadership opens the app, finds the partner, and sees the dates, fees, and obligations laid out in one place.
What changed
A question that used to mean reading a contract now takes a few seconds, and nothing slips by on a renewal or a fee.

Partner Status Newsletter

A recurring status update sent to each partner.

The problem
Our work with each partner moved through a lot of email. Status updates, open items, and requests were spread across threads, so it was hard for anyone, on our side or theirs, to see where an initiative stood or what was still outstanding.
What it does
Every two weeks it gathers the latest status on each partner's initiatives from our internal tracking sources, writes a plain-language summary for that partner, and sends it. Each digest covers where our joint initiatives stand, what we still need from the partner, and what they're waiting on from us.
Who uses it
Our fintech partners, and the internal team that manages them. Partners get a consistent update on a schedule they can count on, and our team stops rebuilding the same status email by hand.
What changed
Status that used to live in scattered email threads became one clear picture, with the items owed on both sides spelled out.

Daily Action-Item Agent

A personalized daily to-do list for the team.

The problem
Our to-dos were scattered across email, our project management software, and our internal chat tools. Everyone had to read through all of it and keep their own running list, which was easy to fall behind on.
What it does
Each morning it scans the previous day's new email, project updates, and chat messages, picks out anything that needs action, works out who it belongs to, and sends each person a short message listing their own items.
Who uses it
My team. Instead of piecing a to-do list together every morning, each teammate starts the day with one already built for them.
What changed
No more tracking to-dos by hand across three tools, and fewer things lost between them.

Predictive analytics · MLB

2027 Bounce-Back & Regression

Which hitters and pitchers are set up to rebound or fall off next year? The idea is simple. Expected stats (xERA, xwOBA) estimate what a player's results should have been based on the quality of their contact, which strips out luck. When a player's actual numbers (ERA, wOBA) drift far from those expected numbers, they tend to snap back the next year. I tested that on six seasons (2021 to 2026), then used 2026 to project 2027.

Built for
A personal project that grew out of my baseball analytics background
How it's used
It ranks which 2026 hitters and pitchers are set up to bounce back or regress in 2027, using luck-adjusted expected stats
What changed
Public Statcast data becomes a focused, luck-adjusted watch list instead of raw season numbers

Does the thesis hold? Pitchers (578 season-pairs, ≥300 BF)

Yes. xERA predicts next year's ERA better than ERA itself, and the gap between actual and expected predicts the next-year change (r = −0.49). Sort 2026 pitchers into groups by how lucky they were and the pattern holds in every group: the luckier the pitcher, the more they fell off the next year.

Next-year ERA change, by 2026 luck

Pitchers in five buckets by their 2026 ERA−xERA gap. Lucky arms (ERA under xERA) got worse; unlucky arms bounced back.

Hitters (994 season-pairs, ≥300 PA)

Same direction, much weaker. Hitting is mostly skill, so a hitter's wOBA is far more stable than a pitcher's ERA and there's less luck to wash out. The bounce-back edge is real but small.

Read it right
This is one signal, not a full projection system. It ignores age, injury, role and ballpark, and the most extreme names often come from small samples. Expected stats have their own error, and the approach works much better for pitchers (ERA has a lot of luck in it) than for hitters. Think of it as a read on who got lucky or unlucky in 2026, something to weigh alongside everything else rather than a firm prediction.

05

DocChat

Retrieval-augmented generation

Upload a PDF and ask questions. DocChat finds the passages most relevant to your question and has Claude answer only from them, with citations. Every answer is grounded in the document, not guessed.

Built for
A personal project to get hands-on with retrieval-augmented generation
How it's used
Upload a PDF, ask a question, and get an answer drawn only from the document with each source passage cited
What changed
Instead of trusting a model's memory, every answer is grounded in the file and traceable to a page

The pipeline

Retrieval runs locally and free; only the final answer calls the model.
PDF→chunk→embed→retrieve top-k→ground→cite

How: the PDF is split into overlapping chunks, and each chunk is turned into a numeric fingerprint (an embedding) by a small model that runs locally. The question gets the same treatment, and the passages whose fingerprints are closest to it are ranked highest. Only the top matches go to Claude, with instructions to answer from them and cite each claim. Because the search step runs locally, the app works without an API key and shows you the passages it found; adding a key turns on the written answers.
